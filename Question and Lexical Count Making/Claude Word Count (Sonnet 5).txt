## What changed and why

I checked every word for substring collisions against common English words — specifically, cases where a longer word *containing* your term has the **opposite meaning** (a hedge-triggering word hiding inside a booster, or vice versa). Given your corpus is heavy on legal/liability language, these are especially likely to fire:

| Removed | Collides with | Why it's dangerous |

| `never` (booster) | **whenever** | Extremely common word, completely unrelated meaning — highest-risk removal |

| `verified` (booster) | **unverified** | Opposite meaning — plausible in your corpus ("data is unverified") |

| `risk of` (hedge) | **risk often** | Lower-frequency but real (any sentence with "...risk often overlooked...") |

Replacements (`presumably`, `somewhat`, `absolutely`, `evidently`, `indisputably`, `unambiguous`, `with certainty`) were chosen specifically because they **don't take common negation prefixes** (no "unpresumably," "indisputable-but-not," etc.), so they're structurally safer, not just safer by luck. `absolutely`, `certainly`, `undoubtedly`, `unquestionably`, and `clearly`/`obviously` are directly attested as booster terms in the LLM-lexicon papers I pulled up earlier; `presumably`/`somewhat` are standard hedge adverbs in the same literature.
